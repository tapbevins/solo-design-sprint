# Test with real people

The step after the synthetic interviews, for when the person wants real evidence. Five people who match the persona use the same prototype while the person watches. It shows whether real people understand the concept and want it. It still does not prove they will pay.

## Who does what

The person finds the people and runs every interview. Claude drafts the screener, the invitation, the script and the grid, and afterwards helps read the notes. Claude never contacts anyone.

Real people stay out of the record. Call them P1 to P5 and keep names, contact details and recordings somewhere else.

## Five people, the right ones

- **Five.** Patterns show by the third or fourth interview, and a sixth adds little.
- **Book six.** About one in six does not turn up. Ask each person to reply to confirm; an accepted calendar invitation is not enough.
- **Matching the persona.** Feedback from the wrong people is worse than none, because it looks like evidence.
- **Not friends, family or colleagues.** They are kind, and kindness hides the problems. Strangers with no loyalty to the person are best.
- **Varied.** Pick the two or three differences that matter most for this concept (for example confidence with technology, or how often they have the problem) and make sure the five differ on them.

## Finding them

Look where the persona already gathers. If the sprint began with a problem hunt, its sources are the first places to ask.

| Where | Good for |
|---|---|
| Community groups and forums the persona uses | people with the problem right now |
| Contacts of contacts (one step removed, never close friends) | quick, and usually free |
| An existing audience: a mailing list, customers of something related | people who already trust the person a little; their reasons may differ from what is assumed |
| Meet-ups, clubs and places the persona goes | meeting people in the setting where the problem happens |
| A paid research panel | speed, when nothing else reaches them |

Offer something fair for their time, say what it is in the invitation, and give it straight after the session, including to anyone who stops early. Enough to be worth their time and not so much that people bend their answers to qualify.

## The screener

A short form that takes two minutes and filters out people who do not fit. Three to five questions:

- Ask what people **do**, not what they think: "How often do you…?", "What do you use for this today?", "When did you last…?"
- Do not give away the answer being looked for. Offer a list of choices where the right one is not obvious.
- Include one question that rules people out (for example, already using the thing being tested, or working in the same field as the person).
- Ask whether they are free at the planned times.

Decide what counts as a pass before reading any replies.

## Setting up

- A video call or a room, 30 to 45 minutes each, with the interviewee driving the prototype. If they cannot share their screen, the person drives and the interviewee says what to do.
- Leave a gap after each interview to write notes while they are fresh.
- Ask before recording. Collect only what is needed and delete recordings once the notes are written up.
- Try the call and the prototype the day before.
- If possible, test where the problem really happens. Reactions are more reliable the closer the setting is to real life.

## The interview, in five parts

**1. Welcome** (a few minutes). "Thanks for doing this. I'm testing an idea, not you; there are no wrong answers, and if something is confusing that's the idea's fault. Please think out loud as you go. Is it all right if I record this for my own notes?"

**2. Today** (5 minutes). How they handle the problem now. "Tell me about the last time you…", "What do you use for it?", "What's annoying about that?"

**3. First look** (5 minutes). "I didn't design this, so say what you really think." Show the first screen. "Before you touch anything: what is this? What could you do here? Who is it for?" Explain nothing. If they are confused, let the silence sit; the confusion is the finding.

**4. Tasks** (15 minutes). Give situations, not instructions.

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| "Click the sign-up button" | "A friend mentioned this and you want to try it" |
| "Go to the pricing page" | "You like it but need to know whether it fits your budget" |

When they get stuck:

| What happens | Say |
|---|---|
| They go quiet | "What are you thinking right now?" |
| They are stuck | "What would you expect to happen?" |
| They ask for help | "What would you try if I weren't here?" |
| They hit a part that is faked | "In the real thing that would work. Let me take you to the next screen." |
| They ask "Am I doing this right?" | "There's no right or wrong. What would you try?" |

**5. Debrief** (5 minutes). "What did you make of it overall?", "Who is this for?", "Would you use it? Why, or why not?", "How does it compare with what you use today?", "If you could change one thing, what would it be?" Then the three sprint questions as plain questions. If everything has been praise: "If a friend asked you about this, what would you warn them about?"

## Staying neutral

| Do | Do not |
|---|---|
| Nod, say "mm-hmm" | Say "great" or "that's right" |
| "Tell me more about that" | "Actually, you're meant to click here" |
| Wait five seconds before speaking | Fill a silence with an explanation |
| Repeat their words back: "You said it felt confusing?" | Defend the design: "Well, the idea is…" |

## Reading the results

Use the same grid as the synthetic interviews: one row for the first look, each task and each sprint question; one column per person; each cell ✓, ✗ or ~ with a few words and, where there is one, a quote.

| Across five people | Read it as |
|---|---|
| 5 the same | strong; act on it |
| 3 or 4 the same | a real signal; look at the odd ones out |
| 2 the same | unclear; not enough to act on |
| 1 dramatic reaction | one voice; wait for a pattern |
| mostly ~ | nobody cares, which is worse news than a ✗ |

Do the reading the same day as the last interview. Then:

1. Answer the three sprint questions again: yes, no, partly or unclear, each with its evidence. Where the real answer differs from the simulated one, the real one stands; say what the simulation got wrong.
2. List what worked, what failed and what split people, with quotes.
3. Decide the next step:

| Result | Next step |
|---|---|
| Concept understood and wanted, small problems | build it, fixing those problems |
| Concept wanted, but people struggled to use it | change the storyboard and prototype, test again |
| Concept not wanted, but something useful learned | go back to stage 4 with what was learned |
| Nothing landed | go back to stage 1 and reframe; a week was spent, not months |
| Unclear | rewrite the tasks or test with five more |

Add the grid, the answers and the decision to the sprint record under "Real-people test", and mark it as real.
